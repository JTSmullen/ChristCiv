# Essay 1 — Christian Doctrine of Creation

## Exact Prompt

Drawing on St. Athanasius, St. Basil, and lecture, explain some of the crucial claims Christians make in the doctrine of creation. This should include a discussion of the cosmos in general and mankind in particular.

## Thesis

Christianity teaches that God freely created a good and ordered cosmos from nothing, while mankind has a special place because human beings are made in God's image and called to become like him.

## Skeleton

`ex nihilo → Creator/creature distinction → creation through Word → goodness → ordered cosmos → image of God → rationality/dominion → image vs likeness → freedom → dependence on God`

## Major Points

### Creation from nothing

- `creatio ex nihilo` means creation from nothing.
- God did not merely organize eternally existing matter.
- God alone is uncreated.
- Everything else receives existence from God.

### Creator/creature distinction

- God is not simply another being within the universe.
- Creation depends upon God.
- God does not depend upon creation.

### Creation through the Word

- Athanasius says God creates through the Word/Logos.
- The Word gives creation order and intelligibility.
- The same Word who creates can later restore creation.

### Creation is good

- Creation is an act of God's goodness.
- God does not create because he lacks something.
- Matter is not intrinsically evil.

### Cosmos is ordered

- Creation is not uncontrolled chaos.
- Its order reflects the rational Word.

### Humanity in God's image

- Humans are creatures but possess distinctive dignity.
- Athanasius connects the image with rationality.
- Humans are capable of knowing/contemplating God.
- Humanity's proper life is oriented toward God.

### Basil: rationality and dominion

- Humans exercise dominion through rationality.
- Proper dominion includes ruling one's own passions.
- Self-rule is part of proper human excellence.

### Image vs likeness

- Image is given in creation.
- Likeness is something humans grow toward through virtue.

### Freedom

- Humans may turn toward or away from God.
- Freedom makes genuine virtue and love possible.
- Turning away from God means turning away from the source of life.
- This leads toward corruption and death.

## Conclusion

Humans are embodied, rational, free creatures made by God, dependent upon God, and created to know and resemble God.

## Recall Questions

- Define `creatio ex nihilo`.
- What is the Creator/creature distinction?
- Why does Athanasius emphasize creation through the Word?
- Why is the goodness of creation important?
- What does image of God mean for Athanasius?
- How does Basil connect dominion and self-rule?
- Contrast image and likeness.
- Why does freedom make sin possible?

# Essay 2 — Basil on Sin and Evil

## Exact Prompt

Explain St. Basil's account of the nature, source, and scope of sin/evil.

## Thesis

Evil is not something God created; it is a corruption or privation of the good caused by misuse of creaturely freedom, and moral evil must be distinguished from suffering and physical misfortune.

## Skeleton

`God is good → evil not substance → privation/corruption → reject dualism → free will → disordered powers → responsibility → Satan → moral evil vs suffering → suffering may correct`

## Major Points

### Problem

If God created everything and God is good, where does evil come from?

### Nature of evil

- Evil is not an independently existing substance.
- Evil is a privation, corruption, or departure from proper goodness.

### Darkness analogy

- Darkness is an absence of light.
- Evil similarly represents a loss or corruption of good.

### Reject dualism

- Good and evil are not two equal eternal powers.
- God alone is ultimate.

### Source of evil

- Moral evil comes from misuse of free will.
- God creates the capacity to choose.
- God does not cause the sinful choice.
- Rational creatures remain responsible.

### Disordered powers

- Human desires and capacities are not inherently evil.
- Sin occurs when good capacities become disordered.
- A lesser good may be chosen over the proper good.

### Satan

- Satan need not have been created evil.
- A good rational creature can become evil through choice.
- Temptation does not eliminate human responsibility.

### Scope of evil

Distinguish:

#### Moral evil

- sin
- vice
- corruption of the person/soul

#### Physical suffering/misfortune

- illness
- pain
- poverty
- disaster
- death

Physical suffering is not automatically moral evil.

### Suffering can serve a purpose

Some painful events may serve as:

- correction
- discipline
- warning
- medicine

This does not make God the author of wickedness.

## Conclusion Formula

`nature = privation`

`source = misuse of freedom`

`scope = distinguish moral evil from physical suffering`

## Recall Questions

- Why can't evil be a created substance?
- Explain the darkness analogy.
- What is dualism?
- Why does Basil reject dualism?
- How does free will explain evil?
- Are desires inherently evil?
- How can Satan become evil?
- Contrast moral evil and suffering.
- How can suffering sometimes serve a good purpose?

# Essay 3 — Athanasius's Two Divine Dilemmas

## Exact Prompt

Explain St. Athanasius's two arguments (i.e. his answer to the two divine dilemmas) of why the Word became flesh. Your explanation should make clear why each dilemma seems like a dilemma, and how Jesus's incarnation, life, and so on is a fitting solution to the aforementioned dilemmas.

## Thesis

The Incarnation solves two problems created by the Fall: humanity is being destroyed by corruption and death, and humanity has lost knowledge of God.

## Skeleton

`creation → fall → corruption/death → truthfulness vs goodness → repentance insufficient → incarnation → death/resurrection → knowledge problem → visible idols → invisible Word becomes visible → works reveal God`

## Background

- God creates humans from nothing.
- Humans receive life and rationality through the Word.
- Turning away from God leads toward death.
- Humanity sins.
- Humanity falls into corruption.

## Dilemma 1 — Death and Corruption

### God's truthfulness

- God warned that rebellion leads to death.
- God cannot simply make his own warning meaningless.

### God's goodness

- Humanity is God's creation.
- Humans bear God's image.
- It would be unfitting for God's creation simply to disappear into corruption.

### Apparent dilemma

If God allows death:

- humanity is destroyed.

If God simply ignores death:

- God's truthfulness appears compromised.

## Why repentance alone is insufficient

- Repentance can change future behavior.
- Repentance does not automatically reverse corruption.
- The problem is deeper than a bad legal record.
- Human nature is dying.

## Solution to Dilemma 1

- The Word who created humanity becomes human.
- The Creator can re-create human nature.
- Christ assumes a real human body.
- Christ undergoes death.
- Death is therefore taken seriously.
- The divine Word cannot ultimately be held by death.
- Christ rises.
- Death is defeated from within.

### Result

God's truthfulness is preserved.

God's goodness is preserved.

## Dilemma 2 — Knowledge of God

- Humans were created to know God.
- Humans turned away from the invisible God.
- Humans became obsessed with visible/sensible things.
- Humans worship idols and created things.
- Humanity therefore loses true knowledge of God.

### Apparent dilemma

Humans were created to know God.

But humans are now spiritually blind and focused on visible things.

## Solution to Dilemma 2

The invisible Word becomes visible.

God meets humanity at the level toward which humanity has turned.

Jesus reveals God through:

- teaching
- miracles
- power over creation
- victory over demons
- resurrection

Visible works lead humans back toward recognition of the divine Word and knowledge of the Father.

## Comparison

### Dilemma 1

Problem:

`corruption + death`

Solution:

`Incarnation + death + resurrection`

### Dilemma 2

Problem:

`ignorance + idolatry`

Solution:

`visible Word + teaching + miracles + resurrection`

## Conclusion Line

The Creator becomes creature to re-create us; the invisible Word becomes visible so we can know God; the immortal Word enters death to destroy death.

## Recall Questions

- What are the two divine dilemmas?
- Why does God's truthfulness create part of dilemma 1?
- Why does God's goodness create part of dilemma 1?
- Why isn't repentance enough?
- Why is the Word the fitting one to restore humanity?
- How does resurrection solve the first dilemma?
- What happened to human knowledge of God?
- Why is becoming visible fitting?
- How do Christ's works reveal God?

# Essay 4 — Theological Virtue of Hope

## Exact Prompt

Explain the theological virtue of hope. What is it and why does it make good sense as a response to the reality of our situation? Make sure to compare and contrast hope with despair and presumption.

## Thesis

Christian hope is confident desire for eternal life and union with God based on God's grace and promises; it avoids both despair and presumption.

## Skeleton

`faith/hope/love → theological virtue → future difficult possible good → eternal life/Kingdom → God's grace/promises → suffering/sin/death → despair → presumption → hope between extremes → action`

## Theological virtues

- faith
- hope
- love

They are called theological because they:

- relate directly to God
- depend on God's grace
- ultimately have God as their object

## Structure of hope

Hope concerns something:

- good
- future
- difficult
- possible

## Christian object of hope

- eternal life
- union with God
- fulfillment of God's Kingdom
- salvation

## Basis of hope

Hope is not primarily:

> I am strong enough.

Hope rests upon:

- God's goodness
- God's power
- God's faithfulness
- God's promises
- God's grace

## Why hope fits reality

Christianity recognizes:

- sin
- weakness
- suffering
- injustice
- death

Therefore Christian hope is not simple optimism.

The resurrection means those realities are serious but not final.

## Despair

Despair says the desired good is impossible.

Examples:

- "My sins cannot be forgiven."
- "God cannot save me."
- "God will not save me."

Despair sees human weakness but forgets or rejects divine grace.

Sadness or grief alone is not necessarily despair.

## Presumption

Presumption treats salvation as guaranteed for the wrong reason.

### Form 1 — self-reliance

> I can save myself.

### Form 2 — abusing God's mercy

> God will save me regardless of how I respond.

This may involve expecting:

- forgiveness without repentance
- salvation without transformation

## Hope Between the Extremes

Against despair:

> Salvation is possible.

Against presumption:

> Salvation is not automatic or self-produced.

Hope says:

> I cannot save myself, but God's grace makes salvation possible, so I faithfully pursue it.

## Hope produces action

Hope motivates:

- perseverance
- repentance
- prayer
- love
- moral growth

Hope is not passive.

## Recall Questions

- Why are faith, hope, and love theological virtues?
- What four features characterize hope?
- What is the object of Christian hope?
- Why isn't Christian hope optimism?
- Define despair.
- Define the two types of presumption.
- How does hope differ from both?
- Why does hope motivate action?

# Essay 5 — Willard's Gospels of Sin Management

## Exact Prompt

Explain what Dallas Willard means by "the gospels of sin management." Explain the gospel of sin management on the right and the gospel of sin management on the left. What, according to Willard, is the true gospel? Make sure to explain differences in key ideas, such as human need, salvation, eternal life, faith, and, of course, the Kingdom of God.

## Thesis

Willard argues that conservative and liberal Christianity can both reduce Christianity to managing sin, while Jesus's true gospel announces that life in God's Kingdom is available now through trusting and following him.

## Skeleton

`sin management → right: guilt/forgiveness/heaven/belief/future Kingdom → critique → left: oppression/social salvation/ideals/political Kingdom → critique → shared mistake → present Kingdom → transformation → faith as trust → discipleship → eternal life now`

## Define Gospels of Sin Management

These are versions of Christianity focused mainly on handling the consequences of sin rather than transforming the whole person into a disciple of Jesus.

---

## Gospel on the Right

### Human need

Guilt before God.

### Salvation

- forgiveness
- getting into heaven after death

### Jesus

Primarily the one whose death handles guilt.

### Faith

May become merely:

- accepting correct doctrine
- believing the correct facts
- accepting an atonement formula

### Eternal life

Can become:

> endless life after death.

### Kingdom of God

Often pushed primarily into the future.

## Willard's Criticism of the Right

A person may:

- trust Jesus for heaven
- not trust Jesus with everyday life

Discipleship becomes optional.

Salvation becomes disconnected from transformation.

---

# Gospel on the Left

## Human need

- oppression
- inequality
- injustice
- harmful social structures

## Salvation

Creating a more just society.

## Jesus

Can become mainly:

- moral example
- symbol of compassion
- symbol of justice
- symbol of liberation

## Faith

Commitment to Jesus-like ideals.

## Kingdom

Can become an ideal social or political order.

## Willard's Criticism of the Left

Justice is important.

The problem is not caring about justice.

The problem occurs when:

- Jesus becomes only a moral symbol
- Christianity becomes mainly a humanitarian or political program

---

# Shared Error

The right manages:

`personal guilt`

The left manages:

`social consequences of sin`

Neither necessarily transforms the sinner into a disciple.

---

# Willard's True Gospel

The Kingdom of God is available now through Jesus.

## Kingdom of God

God's effective rule:

> where what God wants done is done.

The Kingdom is:

- present now
- ultimately fulfilled in the future

## Human Need

Humans need:

- forgiveness
- reconciliation
- transformation

## Salvation

Salvation means:

- entering life with God now
- becoming increasingly Christlike
- continuing in life with God eternally

## Faith

Faith is actual trust or reliance upon Jesus.

## Disciple / Apprentice

A disciple is someone learning from Jesus how to live his own life.

## Eternal Life

Eternal life is interactive knowledge/life with God.

It begins now and continues beyond death.

---

# Three-Way Comparison

## Right

Need:
- guilt

Salvation:
- forgiveness + heaven

Faith:
- belief/acceptance

Eternal life:
- mainly after death

Kingdom:
- mainly future

## Left

Need:
- oppression/injustice

Salvation:
- social liberation

Faith:
- commitment to ideals

Eternal life:
- often de-emphasized

Kingdom:
- ideal society

## Willard

Need:
- reconciliation + transformation

Salvation:
- life with God now and forever

Faith:
- actual trust in Jesus

Eternal life:
- knowledge of God beginning now

Kingdom:
- God's effective rule available now and fulfilled later

## Recall Questions

- Define a gospel of sin management.
- What human problem does the right emphasize?
- How does the right define salvation?
- How can faith become reduced on the right?
- What problem does the left emphasize?
- How can the Kingdom become reduced on the left?
- What mistake do both share?
- Define Willard's Kingdom of God.
- What is salvation for Willard?
- What does faith mean?
- What is a disciple/apprentice?
- When does eternal life begin?

# Essay 6 — Four Interpretations of the Beatitudes

## Exact Prompt

Compare and contrast the four interpretations of the Beatitudes (prescriptive, in spite of, anti-Pharisaical, and spectrum) and explain Ten Elshof's critique of the prescriptive and in-spite-of interpretations. Which one do you find most persuasive and why? Explain what Willard takes the main point of Jesus's Beatitudes to be.

## Thesis

The four interpretations differ over how the states named in the Beatitudes relate to blessing; the spectrum interpretation best handles their diversity while preserving Willard's insight that God's Kingdom is available even to people society does not consider "well off."

## Skeleton

`why blessed? → prescriptive → problem: mourning/persecution → in-spite-of → grace → Elshof critique: positive virtues → anti-Pharisaical → spectrum → defend spectrum → Willard: who is well off? → Kingdom available`

## Interpretive Question

The Beatitudes open the Sermon on the Mount.

The major question is:

> What relationship does the named condition have to being blessed?

---

# 1. Prescriptive Interpretation

The Beatitudes describe virtues or requirements to cultivate.

Essentially:

> Become like this and you will be blessed.

Works reasonably well for:

- merciful
- pure in heart
- peacemakers
- hunger for righteousness

## Problem

It works poorly for:

- mourning
- persecution
- other painful states

It would be strange to say Christians should seek tragedy so they can be blessed.

---

# 2. In-Spite-Of Interpretation

Associated strongly with Willard.

The person is blessed **despite** the listed condition.

The Kingdom is available even to:

- spiritually unimpressive people
- grieving people
- powerless people
- persecuted people

## Strength

Emphasizes grace.

The Kingdom is not something one earns by becoming spiritually impressive.

---

# Ten Elshof's Critique

## Critique of Prescriptive

Not every Beatitude describes something worth pursuing.

Examples:

- mourning
- persecution

Therefore treating every item as a requirement is strained.

## Critique of Strict In-Spite-Of

Not every listed characteristic is a disadvantage.

Examples:

- mercy
- purity of heart
- peacemaking
- hunger for righteousness

These really are good qualities.

It therefore sounds strange to say someone is blessed:

> in spite of being merciful.

---

# 3. Anti-Pharisaical Interpretation

Jesus challenges conventional ideas about who counts as righteous or favored by God.

Emphasizes:

- inward condition
- genuine righteousness
- God's evaluation

rather than:

- religious prestige
- visible achievement
- outward performance alone

Connect this with the rest of the Sermon on the Mount:

- murder → anger
- adultery → lust
- outward religious actions → inner motives

---

# 4. Spectrum Interpretation

Different Beatitudes function differently.

Some describe unfortunate circumstances.

Examples:

- mourning
- persecution

Others describe genuine virtues.

Examples:

- mercy
- purity
- peacemaking

There is no requirement that every Beatitude work according to the exact same formula.

All contribute to one larger announcement about God's Kingdom.

## Why Spectrum Is Strong

It preserves Willard's central insight without requiring positive virtues to become disadvantages.

It handles the diversity of the Beatitudes naturally.

---

# Recommended Position

The spectrum view is the easiest to defend.

Reason:

- Prescriptive is too rigid.
- Strict in-spite-of is also too rigid.
- Spectrum recognizes that different Beatitudes do different things.
- They still form a unified Kingdom announcement.

---

# Willard's Main Point

The Beatitudes ask something like:

> Who is really well off?

Society often assumes the blessed are:

- wealthy
- powerful
- successful
- admired
- attractive
- religiously impressive

Jesus overturns these assumptions.

The Kingdom of God is available even to people society considers:

- hopeless
- powerless
- insignificant
- unfortunate
- spiritually unimpressive

Their suffering does not become good in itself.

Rather, access to God's Kingdom changes what is possible for them.

- Define in-spite-of.
- What is the strength of Willard's reading?
- What is Ten Elshof's criticism of strict in-spite-of?
- Define anti-Pharisaical.
- Define spectrum.
- Why is spectrum persuasive?
- What does Willard think is the main point of the Beatitudes?
